Source: the independent review of #422 (T-0792), non-blocking finding (f), two weaknesses of the link and invite flows that no test can close, because they are about who proves what:
1. Fixation into the attacker's IdP session. An insider who fixates a victim's browser into the attacker's IdP session can link the attacker's IdP subject to the victim's staff account: the victim passes the Kesher step-up, the IdP then authenticates whoever holds the browser's IdP session. The org identity port cannot send `prompt=login` or `max_age`, so the IdP is never forced to re-authenticate.
2. An invite proves nothing about the invitee's IdP identity. A leaked invite plus any signed-in account becomes staff with the invite's role, linked to whatever IdP subject signs in. Admin roles still need the second-admin grant, which bounds the damage to non-admin roles.
Options:
- A. Port change: add `prompt` and `maxAge` to the start input, send `prompt=login` and `max_age=0` on link starts (WorkOS supports passing the OIDC parameters through; verify per connection type, SAML has `ForceAuthn`). Fixes 1 only where the IdP honours it.
- B. A confirmation step at the callback: after the IdP proof, show the member the IdP identity (masked) and require a fresh step-up before the link is written. Fixes 1 without any IdP support; costs one more screen and one more contract change.
- C. Bind the invite: the inviter records an expected email domain (or the exact IdP subject, if the institution can supply it) and the callback refuses a different one. Fixes 2; needs an invite schema field (a migration) and an inviter UI change.
- D. Accept and document both for the pilot (small institutions, a trusted insider is already inside the trust boundary), with an alarm on every link written and on every invite acceptance through SSO.
Recommendation to weigh, not a decision: B for 1 and C (domain, not subject) for 2, with D's alarms regardless.
